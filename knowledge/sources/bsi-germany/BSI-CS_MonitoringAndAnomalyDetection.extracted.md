RECOMMENDATION: IT IN PRODUCTION
Monitoring and Anomaly 
Detection in Production Networks
Is this normal?
Today’s modern production networks feature a topology comparable to conventional IT 
networks and increasingly use protocols that are also based on TCP1/IP2. These protocols 
are addressed not only by intelligent control devices; sensors and actuators are using 
them to communicate more and more often, as well. The sheer number of such com-
ponents and the connections among them are resulting in networks of ever greater 
complexity. Industrial network elements and segments are connected by switches, while 
firewalls and other monitoring solutions are deployed to safeguard the edges of network 
segments and the gateways that link them. In this context, monitoring involves logging 
and analysing the data and flows thereof in a given network, which mainly aids in 
identifying anomalies that can impact technical and business concerns. Functionality, 
quality, availability, safety3 and security4 are some examples of the areas that can be af-
fected. The industrial networks of today are similar in structure (and often connected) to 
office IT networks. The threats this presents, which are comparable to those faced in 
conventional IT, require a focus on security. While using IT components from tradi-
tional office settings in production networks offers advantages in terms of costs, opera-
tions and user-friendliness, they also present more points of attack [see[1]]. This makes 
monitoring and anomaly detection essential with regard to prevention, detection and 
response.
This cyber security recommendation explains the basic principles of monitoring and 
anomaly detection and also offers assistance in selecting related products.
1 Monitoring
Monitoring is a collective term for all types of direct and systematic identification, ob-
servation, or supervision of a process using technical resources or other corresponding 
systems (see [2]).

In information technology, monitoring involves continuous observation of processes, devices, 
communication links and services within a given network. Along with the data transmitted 
over the network, this includes the log data [3] generated by network components (such as 
switches or firewalls). Monitoring is implemented by means of appropriate hard- and software.
In industrial environments, and thus in the context of this publication, monitoring is not lim-
ited to mere status monitoring (conditions, processes, alarms). It also covers the observation of 
communications transmitted among automation components, to actuators and sensors, and 
to and from remote components. This involves triggering notifications and alarms when 
events of significance are detected, as well. In addition, management and control systems such 
as MES5, ERP6and historians must be taken into account.
2 How does Monitoring work?
In most cases, monitoring is an independent process that is meant to oversee all the devices in 
a given network regardless of their type, purpose and manufacturer. Data and information are 
collected in one or more central locations (or requested from a central location), analysed us-
ing technical procedures and presented in the form of status reports. Monitoring solutions also 
make it possible to establish thresholds, which means that such status reports can range from 
simple signals (red or green lights once a threshold has been reached, for example) to complex 
diagrams, statistics and risk assessments. This helps ensure network transparency.
To make efficient use of monitoring, it is necessary not only to choose a suitable solution, but 
to analyse the processes to be monitored and define corresponding criteria, as well.
3 Anomalies
Anomalies are unexpected deviations from corresponding rules (see [4]) – or, in the context of 
production, from “normal operating conditions”. They usually arise when an error occurs, but 
can also be an indication of an attack or a form of manipulation within a production network. 
This is particularly true when events happen for the first time, processes exhibit abnormal be-
haviour, or devices begin communicating with one another despite not having done so before. 
Analysing anomalies thus makes it possible to detect attack patterns, including those that are 
not yet known. This makes anomaly detection a suitable technique for determining opera-
tional conditions, generating warnings and performing more effective forensics when neces-
sary. Anomaly detection is one of the basic methods of intrusion detection (see[5]). That said, it 
is not a static system that acts based on fixed or known threat patterns (like indexed computer 
viruses, for example). Instead, anomaly detection continually re-evaluates the standard com-
munications of the network at hand, which facilitates dynamic adjustments to changes in 
threat vectors. This in turn enables such systems to detect behaviour patterns that have not yet 
been seen in the network or registered on virus or error status lists.

4 What are anomalies?
In production networks, anomalies are a dynamic category of potentially disruptive factors. 
They include the following factors, among others:
• Unusual or exceptional activities in a network (including ICS7)
◦ Connection of a new device
◦ DHCP8 requests
◦ Data packets from an as-yet unknown device
◦ Data transmission between devices that have not previously communicated
◦ Data transmission via a protocol that has not been used before
◦ Data transmission via an unusual protocol or one not intended for the purpose at hand
◦ Events that occur at unusual times
◦ Use of unexpected addresses (public IP addresses, etc.)
◦ Generally noteworthy events such as address or port scans
◦ Changes in network quality, including high broadband usage, increased round-trip 
times and smaller TCP window sizes
• Abnormal events in logs typical of production environments (including ICS)
◦ Unusual error messages
◦ Unsupported function calls
◦ Function calls that have not been used before
◦ Flawed data packets
◦ Unknown function codes
◦ Abnormal protocol behaviour
◦ Unexpected transition from one protocol to another
• Unusual changes in process data (sensor data, control data, etc.)
◦ Values outside of defined ranges
◦ Changes in frequency
◦ Changes in cycle times
◦ Changing variance within certain periods of time
5 How does Anomaly Detection work?
To identify anomalies, the normal state of a system – in this case, a production network – must 
first be known. To that end, passive sensors (ideally, passive network or wire taps) are intro-
duced to the network or network segment at hand as a means of collecting data on the net-
work over an extended period of time (the “training phase”). Alternatively, a switch with port 
mirroring can also be used. In this context, data includes all the IT-related signals transmitted 
on the network (usage and logging data, packet data, and so on). The system gathers all the in-
formation that arises, which enables it to learn the network’s topology, the communication

The data acquired can be analysed, categorised and evaluated based on a wide variety of cri-

In accordance with its purpose, a detection system needs to be integrated into the network to 
be monitored as an independent, stand-alone component that has interfaces to signalling, no-

6 Required Features of Anomaly Detection Systems
In accordance with the examples of anomalies presented in Section 4, it is possible to define 
several categories of requirements that an anomaly detection system should meet to the 
greatest extent possible.
6.1 Category: general requirements
• Overview of all devices that communicate in the network
• Identification of all the protocols used in the network
• Identification of all the communication links in the network
• Monitoring of network load over time (data volume, communication time, etc)
• Configuration of adjustable detection criteria
• Definition of multiple escalation levels
• Adjustable, clearly arranged means of visualisation
• User-friendly notification and presentation of events
• Presentation of event correlations
• Support for integration into existing signalling, notification and alarm systems
• Scalability (e.g. support for additional storage capacity)
• Statistics
• Filter functions
• Export functions for further analysis (e.g. anomaly-specific network records, including for 
forensics where applicable)
• A model of users and roles for system access
• Support for updates
• Internal logging functions

6.2 Category: unusual or exceptional activities in a network (including ICS)
Basic requirements:
• Identification of new devices in ICS networks
• Identification of communications taking place between two devices that have not previ-

• Identification of communications taking place between two devices via a TCP/UDP9 port

• Identification of new protocols or changes in protocol among individual components
• Identification of connections to insecure networks (e.g. the public Internet)
• Identification of insecure communication attributes (e.g. a lack of encryption)
Additional requirements:
• Identification of fluctuations in data volume and frequency
• Presentation of unusual activities
• Detailed presentations (with drill-down options10 where applicable)
• Assessment of the relevance and risk presented by detected anomalies
6.3 Category: abnormal events in logs typical of production environments (including ICS)

• Identification of error messages, including (for example):
◦ Invalid or unsupported function calls or function codes
◦ Invalid addresses or destinations

• The ability to determine whether an access attempt (e.g. read/write) pertains to an address

7 Active or passive?
Active and passive solutions for monitoring and anomaly detection differ in terms of whether 
they collect the data required non-interacting (passive) or create data themselves in the mon-
itored network (active). The latter can take place by means of a solution’s own requests in the 
network; mechanisms for detecting new participants in the network; or active querying of sys-
tem conditions, firmware versions and log data. The terms “active” and “passive” refer in partic-
ular to the sensors used for data collection and system connectivity in a given network.
7.1 Active systems
Active systems produce data transmissions in the network to be monitored. Such transmis-
sions contain (for example):
• Log data queries
• Queries regarding device types, firmware versions, system states and measurement data
• Pings or ARP requests meant to check a network segment for current participants
As a rule, active systems rarely require interventions in a given network, if they do at all; they 
are incorporated via existing access points. The mirror ports of existing switches can also be 
used for collection purposes. The system at hand must then be configured such that the data it 
generates itself can be distinguished from the data to be monitored. Effects on time-related be-
haviour in the network segment in question may also need to be taken into account. An at-
tacker capable of manipulating data in a given network can, of course, also influence the mon-
itoring system in place. Meanwhile,  production networks are often time-sensitive, where data 
needs to be transmitted according to defined time slots. Additional data packets can lead to de-
viations and cause workflow disruptions. In typical heterogeneous production networks where 
some firmware and hardware has not been updated in a very long time, active querying can it-
self pose a risk to stability; this should therefore be considered, as well. In addition, active sys-
tems should support the protocols of other active devices in the network at hand and must not 
adversely affect the warranties of the equipment present.
7.2 Passive systems
Passive systems make use of network or wire taps in a manner that has no effect on data or 
time-sensitive behaviour in the network at hand. They are also not used to transmit data into 
the network. In some situations, the network itself or a particular network path must be se-
gregated in order to install network or wire taps. As a result, it should only be done at an appro-
priate time, such as prior to commissioning or during a maintenance window. Devices cannot 
be queried using passive network sensors.
8 Conclusion
The terms “monitoring” and “anomaly detection” are typically used interchangeably. Upon 
closer inspection, however, they are two activities that build on one another. Indeed, monitor-
ing establishes a foundation on which anomalies can be identified during further observation. 
This means that the differences between the two activities, which largely employ the same 
methods and mechanisms, are not clearly defined.

Monitoring first provides for transparency with regard to the participants and communication 
links in a given production network, which enables it to serve general commissioning and 
maintenance purposes, as well. As an oversight solution, monitoring is a suitable means of 
checking whether processes meet defined standards of behaviour and identifying deviations 
from established patterns.
Anomaly detection, meanwhile, is a tool for protecting all types of networks. It makes it pos-
sible to identify atypical behaviour, which is useful not only in connection with technical er-
rors and flawed configurations, but in detecting as-yet unknown forms of network attacks. 
This sets anomaly detection apart from other measures that rely on the identification of 
threats that are already known.
Along with technical systems for monitoring and anomaly detection, other technical and 
organisational processes are necessary to evaluate the results and notifications such systems 
provide and take appropriate action in response.
Due to the apparent increase in attacks on networks in the realms of production and critical 
infrastructure, ways to detect incursions like these are more essential than ever. At the same 
time, such measures have to accommodate complex structures, which is why they require cor-
responding systems. Monitoring and anomaly detection should not, however, be implemented 
as stand-alone security solutions, but in combination with other protective measures. In ac-
cordance with ISO/IEC 27001[6] [7] (and thus with ISO/IEC 62443-4-2[8]), monitoring is a com-
ponent of "information and security management systems" (ISMS).
